04
Main idea

From event detection to stress assessment

Traditional question

Did bleaching happen?

  • Often based on one main thermal metric, especially DHW.
  • Focuses on whether a threshold was crossed.
  • Very useful for operational warning systems.
  • But it gives limited information about which environmental conditions produced the stress.
This study

How stressed is the reef, and why?

  • Uses a 26-week environmental history, not a single day.
  • Combines thermal, optical, and water-quality information.
  • Produces a continuous stress score instead of only alert / no alert.
  • Separates the result into pathways so the stress is more interpretable.
The core idea is simple: bleaching is the visible outcome, but we want one continuous index that describes the environmental stress process leading to that outcome.
After defining this idea, I named the index: Coral Reef Environmental Stress Index (CRESI).

Bleaching concept

What is coral bleaching?

Healthy corals live with microscopic algae, often called zooxanthellae or Symbiodiniaceae, inside their tissue. These symbionts provide much of the coral’s energy through photosynthesis.

What do the labels mean?

BO

Bleached observation

  • The reef showed visible bleaching.
  • Coral tissue looked pale or white.
  • Used as the positive class during training and evaluation.
BX

Non-bleached observation

  • No visible bleaching was reported at that observation.
  • Used as the comparison class.
  • Important for learning what low-stress versus high-stress conditions look like.

07
Current practice

Operational monitoring still relies mainly on heat

What is used now?

  • NOAA Coral Reef Watch provides daily 5-km thermal products.
  • The main thermal products are SST, HotSpot, and DHW.
  • These products are the operational standard for large-scale bleaching alerts.

Why is this not enough by itself?

  • Thermal products focus on heat exposure.
  • But bleaching can also be modified by light, clarity, and water quality.
  • Heat is essential, but it is not always the whole explanation.

08
Previous evidence

Previous studies already show that non-thermal conditions matter

Light / PAR

High light can make bleaching worse

  • Downs et al. (2013): heat-only and light-only each caused damage, but combined heat + light caused both damage pathways.
  • Srisoontorn et al. (2022): high temperature + high light produced strong loss of symbionts and pigments.
  • Meaning: when temperature is already high, strong light can amplify photo-oxidative stress.
Water clarity / Kd(490)

Clarity changes how much light reaches the coral

  • Sully & van Woesik (2020): across 3,694 reef sites, moderate turbidity reduced bleaching during thermal stress.
  • Mechanism: stronger light attenuation means less irradiance reaches the coral, so photodamage can be reduced.
  • Meaning: clearer water can increase light stress, while moderate turbidity can provide natural shading.
Take-home point: temperature is not the only environmental control. The surrounding light field also matters.
09
Previous evidence

Water quality can also modify bleaching susceptibility

Nutrients / water quality

Nutrient imbalance can lower bleaching resistance

  • Donovan et al. (2020): under relatively low heat stress, high nitrogen pollution increased bleaching severity by about twofold.
  • Wiedenmann et al. (2013): nutrient imbalance increased susceptibility to temperature- and light-induced bleaching.
  • Meaning: even at the same temperature, reefs in nutrient-enriched water can bleach more easily.
Chl-a / productivity

Productivity can also play a role

  • Keighan et al. (2023): moderate Chl-a was associated with lower bleaching prevalence during a thermal event.
  • Meaning: not all productivity effects point in one direction. Some conditions may worsen stress, but moderate productivity can sometimes be protective.
  • This is one reason the environmental response should be learned from data, not assumed from one fixed rule.
Take-home point: non-thermal environmental conditions can modify bleaching through different mechanisms.

Gaps and objective

Why do we still need CRESI?

1

Heat alone is incomplete

Accumulated heat is essential, but it cannot explain all observed bleaching responses.

2

Integration is still limited

Thermal, optical, and water-quality effects are often discussed separately rather than combined into one interpretable stress estimate.

3

Validation is needed

Regional coral-stress assessments still need clearer evaluation against independent bleaching observations at reefs.

Study objective

Develop and evaluate a satellite-based environmental stress index for Indonesian coral reefs by integrating thermal, optical, and water-quality histories.

Important naming

  • CRESI = Coral Reef Environmental Stress Index
  • Mamba = temporal model used to read the 26-week sequence
  • CRESI-Mamba = one implementation used to estimate the CRESI stress score

12
Thermal variable 1

SST: Sea Surface Temperature

What is SST?

  • SST is the temperature of the sea surface.
  • In this study, SST comes from NOAA Coral Reef Watch.
  • The product is based on satellite sea-surface temperature analyses, delivered at 5-km daily resolution.
  • Unit: degrees Celsius (°C).

How should we read the values?

  • Higher SST means warmer surface water.
  • Lower SST means cooler surface water.
  • But SST alone does not tell us whether the water is unusually warm for that reef.
Example: 29°C may be normal for one reef, but stressful for another. To know whether it is abnormal, we need an anomaly metric.

Why does SST matter for bleaching?

  • Corals live close to their upper thermal limit.
  • When SST becomes too high, photosynthesis in the symbionts is disrupted.
  • This increases oxidative stress and can lead to bleaching.
13
Thermal variable 2

SSTA: Sea Surface Temperature Anomaly

What is SSTA?

  • SSTA tells us how much the temperature differs from the expected climatological temperature for that place and time.
  • In simple form: SSTA = observed SST − climatological SST.
  • Unit: degrees Celsius (°C).

How do we read it?

  • SSTA > 0: warmer than normal
  • SSTA = 0: close to normal
  • SSTA < 0: cooler than normal
Example: if SST is 29.3°C and the expected climatological value is 28.7°C, then SSTA = +0.6°C.

Why is SSTA useful?

  • It adds local context.
  • The same absolute temperature can mean different stress levels at different reefs.

SSTA is a general anomaly metric. The bleaching threshold used by NOAA for DHW comes from HotSpot, which is tied to the MMM baseline and explained next.
14
Important baseline

MMM and HotSpot: the threshold concept behind DHW

What is MMM?

  • MMM = Maximum Monthly Mean.
  • It is the warmest monthly climatological mean for that location.
  • It represents the typical warm-season upper baseline.

What is HotSpot?

  • HotSpot = SST − MMM
  • If HotSpot is positive, water is above the MMM baseline.
  • For DHW accumulation, only the part above +1°C is counted.
Example: SST = 30.5°C and MMM = 29.0°C → HotSpot = 1.5°C. This contributes to DHW because it is above +1°C.
15
Thermal variable 3

DHW: Degree Heating Weeks

What is DHW?

  • DHW summarizes accumulated heat stress over the previous 12 weeks.
  • Only HotSpot values above +1°C contribute.
  • Unit: °C-weeks.

Simple example

  • Week 1 HotSpot = 1.5°C → counts as 1.5
  • Week 2 HotSpot = 0.7°C → counts as 0
  • Week 3 HotSpot = 2.0°C → counts as 2.0
  • If the 12-week sum is 4.0, then DHW = 4 °C-weeks.
DHWGeneral meaning
0no accumulated thermal stress
< 4heat is building, but below the common bleaching alert threshold
≥ 4possible bleaching risk
≥ 8severe thermal stress, high bleaching/mortality risk
Real study result: many bleaching observations still occurred under low DHW.

16
Optical variable 1

PAR: Photosynthetically Active Radiation

What is PAR?

  • PAR measures the amount of sunlight available for photosynthesis at the sea surface.
  • In this study, PAR comes from NASA VIIRS ocean-color products.
  • Unit: Einstein m-2 day-1 (commonly treated as mol photons m-2 day-1).

How do we read it?

  • High PAR: stronger incoming sunlight
  • Low PAR: weaker incoming sunlight, often due to cloud or season
High PAR is not automatically “bad,” but under high temperature it can amplify photo-oxidative damage.
17
Optical / water-quality link

Kd(490): how quickly does light disappear underwater?

What is Kd(490)?

  • Kd(490) is the diffuse attenuation coefficient at 490 nm.
  • It describes how quickly light intensity decreases with depth.
  • Derived from NASA VIIRS ocean-color products after remote-sensing reflectance processing.
  • Unit: m-1.

How do we read it?

  • Low Kd(490) → clear water, light penetrates deeper
  • High Kd(490) → turbid water, light is attenuated quickly
Example: Kd(490) = 0.05 m-1 is clearer than 0.15 m-1. Clearer water can raise light exposure at the coral, while moderate turbidity can provide shading.
18
Water-quality variable

Chl-a: chlorophyll-a as a proxy for phytoplankton and nutrients

What is Chl-a?

  • Chl-a is the concentration of chlorophyll-a in the water column.
  • Derived from NASA VIIRS ocean-color products.
  • Unit: mg m-3.

What does it tell us?

  • It is a satellite-observable proxy for phytoplankton biomass and broader productivity conditions.
  • Higher Chl-a often suggests more productive or nutrient-influenced water.
  • Lower Chl-a often suggests clearer, more oligotrophic oceanic water.
Example: if one reef has Chl-a = 0.2 mg m-3 and another has 1.5 mg m-3, the second reef is in a much more productive or nutrient-enriched water mass.

Why can Chl-a matter for bleaching?

  • Nutrient-enriched conditions can change coral–symbiont balance and lower stress tolerance.
  • But not all productivity effects are harmful. Some moderate productivity conditions may be protective.
  • That is why the model learns the relationship from data rather than imposing one fixed direction.

Take-home messages

Summary of today’s seminar

Thermal pathway

SST, SSTA, MMM, HotSpot, DHW

  • SST = absolute temperature
  • SSTA = anomaly from climatology
  • MMM + HotSpot define the bleaching threshold logic
  • DHW = accumulated thermal stress over 12 weeks
Optical pathway

PAR and clarity

  • PAR represents incoming sunlight for photosynthesis.
  • Kd(490) describes how fast light disappears underwater.
  • High light can amplify thermal damage.
  • Clear versus turbid water changes the light field experienced by corals.
Water-quality pathway

Kd(490) and Chl-a context

  • Chl-a helps represent productivity and nutrient-related conditions.
  • Water quality can lower or modify bleaching tolerance.
  • This is why CRESI combines multiple pathways rather than relying on heat alone.
Final message: bleaching is the observed endpoint, but CRESI is designed to describe the environmental stress process that leads to it.
